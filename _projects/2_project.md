**Abstract:** How does shock-induced migration transform democratic governance in left behind places? Migration in response to environmental shocks is central to household risk diversification in developing countries, yet its consequences for governance remain poorly understood. I argue that mass migration weakens local accountability by fueling dependence on remittances and draining communities of local leaders. Using India's Rural Economic and Demographic Survey (REDS), I construct a novel panel dataset spanning 600 village term observations to estimate fixed effects models of shock-induced migration and public goods provision. I find an inverted-U relationship—low to moderate migration is associated with small, imprecisely estimated gains in school access, health facilities, electrification, and street lighting, but provision declines once migration crosses a certain threshold. By contrast, the frequency of village council meetings and elected member attendance fall monotonically with migration. These findings are consistent with migration undercutting grassroots accountability mechanisms in left behind regions, with implications for democratic representation and political inequality.

**Key Words:** Migration, Local Institutions, Public Goods Provision, Political Representation, Democratic Accountability, India
